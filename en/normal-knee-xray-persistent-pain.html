Normal Knee X-ray, but Persistent Pain?

“Your X-ray is normal” may be reassuring. But if your knee still hurts, it is reasonable to ask what the image actually rules out. The answer is less clear-cut than a single radiographic grade might suggest.

What does “normal” mean on an X-ray?

X-rays show bones, joint-space width and visible bony outgrowths, but they do not directly show cartilage or the meniscus. The widely used Kellgren–Lawrence scale grades radiographic changes from 0 to 4. Grade 0 means no osteoarthritis features are visible according to that scale. Grade 2 requires a definite osteophyte – a bony outgrowth at the joint margin – for the label of radiographic osteoarthritis.

That is useful for describing images and comparing research groups. It is not, however, a verdict on whether a knee is painful, which tissues are involved, or what an individual needs.

MRI may reveal more – but more is not always the answer

In a population study published in BMJ, 710 people over 50 with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 0 had knee MRI scans. Cartilage damage was reported in 69%, and at least one of several assessed MRI abnormalities in 89%. Such findings were also common among people without knee pain. The study shows that a “normal X-ray” does not mean “no structural changes” – but neither does an MRI abnormality automatically explain pain.

The same caution applies to the meniscus. A large study in the New England Journal of Medicine found that meniscal changes on MRI often occur without symptoms. Imaging needs to be interpreted alongside the onset, location and pattern of symptoms, function and examination findings.

Two mistakes to avoid

A normal X-ray does not prove that the knee is healthy. An MRI finding does not prove that the finding causes the pain. Both are tools, not a diagnosis in isolation.

Is an osteophyte a “good” sign?

A video on this topic argues that osteophytes are mainly a successful stabilising response and that knees without them may therefore be the dangerous ones. It is a provocative interpretation, but the evidence does not support dividing knees into “safe with osteophytes” and “dangerous without”. Osteophytes are part of the joint's remodelling in osteoarthritis; their size alone cannot predict its course. A longitudinal study did not find large osteophytes to be independently protective against further joint-space narrowing. The aim is to avoid both false reassurance and unnecessary alarm.

When is further investigation needed?

For typical osteoarthritis symptoms in people over 45, NICE recommends a clinical diagnosis without routine imaging. MRI is not automatically the next step after a normal X-ray, either. Further imaging may be appropriate when symptoms are atypical, another condition is suspected, or the result would actually change management.

A sudden change in pain and ability to bear weight deserves particular attention. Meniscal root tears and injury to the bone beneath the cartilage can occur early and have been associated with faster osteoarthritis progression in research. This does not mean everyone with sudden knee pain has these conditions; it means that an apparently normal image should not end the assessment when the clinical story does not fit.

What should happen in practice?

Start with the symptoms: when they began, loading, swelling, stiffness, locking, injury and everyday function. A careful clinical examination helps establish whether the pattern fits osteoarthritis, a meniscal or tendon problem, or whether another cause needs consideration. Ultrasound may help assess some superficial structures and fluid in the knee, but it does not replace MRI when deeper cartilage, meniscal or bone injury is suspected. A treatment plan should be based on function and goals, with education, suitable activity and strengthening as the foundation where appropriate.

How I approach it

I explain what an image shows – and what it cannot show. Then I connect the findings with the symptoms and a focused examination. The goal is a clear diagnosis and a practical plan, not to treat an image or dismiss pain because a report says “normal”.
