Cartilage Wear and Knee Pain – Why Imaging Is Not the Whole Answer

Being told that the cartilage in your knee is 'worn out' can make worsening pain seem inevitable. It is not. A knee is a whole organ, and the relationship between imaging, tissues and experienced pain is complex.

If cartilage lacks nerves, what hurts?

Healthy articular cartilage normally has no pain fibres. The joint lining, underlying bone, capsule, ligaments and parts of the meniscus do have sensory innervation. In osteoarthritis, nerve growth and sensitisation may alter responses to loading and inflammatory signals. This does not make cartilage changes irrelevant: they can affect load distribution and nearby tissues.

Studies associate subchondral bone marrow changes and inflammation of the joint lining with knee pain. One longitudinal study linked changes in synovitis with changes in pain; another associated enlarging bone marrow lesions with new symptoms. The strength of these relationships differs between studies and populations.

Why can't MRI give a complete answer?

MRI describes structure, not a person's pain experience. In a BMJ study of adults over 50 with normal osteoarthritis X-rays, cartilage changes were common even without pain. A six-year study of knees without radiographic osteoarthritis found some statistical links between MRI findings and pain, but no single finding corresponded to a clinically important difference. It would be too simple to call one MRI feature the cause for every patient.

The video's point that normal cartilage itself lacks sensory innervation is well founded. Its suggestion that one MRI feature is universally the strongest marker of pain is too categorical. The type of symptom, stage, other tissues and individual pain sensitivity matter.

What does this mean for care?

You do not need to 'fix the scan' before symptoms can improve. Explore when pain occurs, provoking activities, swelling, function and personal goals. For typical osteoarthritis, education, adapted activity and strengthening are central. A major acute change, injury or atypical symptoms call for reassessment.

A clear account of what images can and cannot show can avoid both false reassurance and needless alarm. Pain remains real even when a scan looks relatively unremarkable.

How I approach it

I take pain seriously without treating one MRI observation as a certain explanation. Together we connect symptoms, examination and any imaging to a practical functional goal.
