When PRP Meets Hyaluronic Acid – Is the Combination Better for Knee Osteoarthritis?

PRP delivers a concentrated mixture of platelets and signalling molecules from the patient's own blood. Hyaluronic acid influences the mechanical and biological environment inside the joint. The rationale is that they may complement each other – but does a biologically plausible combination produce better outcomes for patients?

Two different mechanisms – one shared goal

Knee osteoarthritis is more than simply “worn cartilage”. Cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, muscles and load tolerance may all contribute. PRP and hyaluronic acid do not address this environment in the same way. PRP is primarily studied for its biological signalling molecules and possible modulation of inflammation. Hyaluronic acid is used as a viscosupplement and may improve lubrication, shock absorption and aspects of the joint environment.

Laboratory research suggests that a blend can retain relevant biological and mechanical properties. This provides a reasonable hypothesis for synergy, but laboratory findings alone do not prove clinical benefit or cartilage regeneration.

The study behind the question

In the prospective study that prompted this article, 60 people with grade II–III knee osteoarthritis were divided between hyaluronic acid, PRP and PRP + hyaluronic acid. At six months, the combination group had the lowest average pain and WOMAC scores and was the only group that continued to improve between three and six months.

That is an interesting signal, but the study was small – only 20 participants per group – and follow-up lasted six months. It supports further investigation, but cannot by itself establish that the combination is best or identify who should receive it.

What does the wider evidence show?

Several systematic reviews suggest that PRP + hyaluronic acid may improve pain and function more than hyaluronic acid alone. The comparison with PRP alone is less consistent. Some meta-analyses report an advantage for combination treatment at selected time points, while others find no clear difference.

A 2025 review is particularly useful because it considered whether the differences were large enough to matter to patients. Combination treatment produced statistically better total and physical-function scores than PRP alone, but the differences were below the minimal clinically important difference. There was no clear advantage for pain, stiffness or several other outcomes. Minor, self-limiting reactions were somewhat less frequent with combination treatment, although products and protocols varied across studies.

The short answer

Yes, the combination may offer an added benefit – particularly compared with hyaluronic acid alone. Compared with PRP alone, it remains uncertain whether the average difference is large enough for an individual patient to notice. The approach is promising, but not proven to be universally superior.

The right patient matters more than adding more ingredients

The evidence mainly concerns symptomatic knee osteoarthritis, often mild to moderate. It cannot automatically be extrapolated to every joint, tendon disorder or advanced osteoarthritis. Before treatment, the pain source, severity, swelling, function, loading pattern, previous care and the patient's goals should be assessed together. Diagnostic ultrasound can clarify joint fluid and other structures, while radiographs and clinical findings may also be needed.

There is also no single standard PRP product or HA formulation. Platelet concentration, leukocytes, injection volume, molecular weight, number of injections and whether the substances are administered together all vary. This makes both the research and the clinical decision more complex than “PRP or PRP + HA?”

An injection does not replace rehabilitation

International guidance continues to place education, physical activity, strength training, weight management when relevant and individualised rehabilitation at the foundation of knee osteoarthritis care. An injection may be considered when pain limits progress, but it should create a better window for loading and function – not be presented as proof that osteoarthritis has been “repaired”.

How I work

I first establish whether the symptoms fit knee osteoarthritis, which structures contribute and what has already been tried. If an injection is considered, we discuss expected benefit, uncertainty, cost and alternatives. The procedure is targeted and ultrasound-guided, but always sits within a plan for graded activity, strength and measurement of pain and function over time. Medicinal injections are discussed and administered in collaboration with a physician whenever required.
