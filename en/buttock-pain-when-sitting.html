Buttock Pain When Sitting – Why MRI Alone Is Not Enough

Sitting-related buttock pain may feel like one precise point or spread across a larger area. Both experiences are real, but neither pattern gives a certain diagnosis. We need to understand what provokes and eases it, and whether nerve symptoms are present.

Several possible explanations

Pain near the sitting bone, particularly with prolonged sitting and running, may be consistent with proximal hamstring tendinopathy. Deep buttock pain with radiation can sometimes reflect sciatic nerve irritation in the deep gluteal space. Pain on the outside of the hip may instead involve the gluteal tendons. The back and hip joint can also refer pain to the region.

These presentations overlap. A systematic review of deep gluteal syndrome describes history, examination and imaging when indicated as parts of the assessment. Another review found sitting pain among the features reported in piriformis-related presentations. It would therefore be wrong to rule out all muscular or tendon causes in advance.

Connect MRI findings with symptoms

In a study of proximal hamstring MRI, increased signal within the tendon was very common and by itself unrelated to symptoms. Some other findings were associated with symptoms more often, yet could still appear without pain. Imaging can help answer a clinical question; it is not a pain detector.

Assessment should establish the exact location, effects of sitting, walking, running and hip movement, and any changes in strength, sensation or back symptoms. New paralysis, changes in bladder or bowel control, or other serious neurological features warrant prompt medical assessment.

A pain score cannot identify the tissue

The video suggests that 3 out of 10 is not 'real pain', whereas 7 or above points to particular tissues. Research does not support that. A numerical rating describes the person's experienced intensity; it does not distinguish nerve, bone, tendon or muscle pain. Studies also find that boundaries between mild, moderate and severe pain vary.

Adjusted loading and progressive exercise may help when the condition has been properly assessed. In gluteal tendinopathy, education and exercise led to better global improvement than corticosteroid injection at longer follow-up in a randomised trial. That does not mean one exercise plan suits everyone with sitting pain, or that stretching inevitably causes permanent damage.

How I approach it

I use the location of pain as a clue, not a verdict. History, focused tests and any appropriate imaging need to form an explanation that makes sense to the patient.
